How Supplement Brands Choose Their Bottle Size

Two bottles sit side by side on the shelf. One is tall and slim, the other short and wide. One holds 60 capsules, the other holds 90. One costs more, but the other costs more per capsule. The bottle size is not an accident, and it is not only about the product inside. It is a decision about shelf space, shipping cost, psychology, and profit — and understanding it helps a buyer compare products honestly.

The first factor is the dose. A product taken once a day needs 30 capsules for a month's supply; a product taken three times a day needs 90. The bottle is sized to the course: 30, 60, 90 or 120 capsules are the standard counts, and each one implies a month, two months or three months of use. A brand that sells a 30-count bottle of a twice-daily product is selling a two-week supply in a month-sized bottle, and the buyer who does not notice will run out halfway through the month and blame the product for not working.

The second factor is the shelf. Retailers charge brands for shelf space, and a tall, slim bottle takes up less frontage than a short, wide one. A brand that wants its product on a crowded shelf will choose a bottle that fits the retailer's planogram — the diagram that decides which products go where. Online, the same logic applies to the product photograph: a tall bottle looks more substantial in a thumbnail, and a wide bottle looks squat. The shape is chosen for the shelf, not for the supplement.

The third factor is shipping. A bottle is mostly air — the capsules inside take up a fraction of the volume, and the rest is empty space, cotton, and a silica packet. A smaller bottle costs less to ship, less to store, and less to pack. A brand that ships directly to customers has a strong incentive to use the smallest bottle that will hold the product, and to fill it as full as possible. A brand that sells through retailers has the opposite incentive: a larger bottle looks like better value on the shelf, even when the contents are the same. The tension between the two is why the same product can appear in different bottle sizes in different shops.

The fourth factor is psychology. A large bottle feels like a bargain, even when the price per capsule is higher than a smaller bottle of the same product. A bottle that is half full feels like a rip-off, even when the dose is exactly as labelled. Brands know this, and they size their bottles accordingly — sometimes adding a larger bottle, or a cotton wad, to manage the feeling rather than the contents. The honest comparison is always the same: divide the price by the number of capsules, then divide again by the daily dose. The result is the cost per day, and it is the only number that matters.
